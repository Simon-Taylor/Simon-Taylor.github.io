AI in everyday work

Everyone else seems to have AI figured out. They haven't.

Most businesses are at the same point: a few people use it, nobody is sure what it has changed, and the board has started asking. The gains are real, though, and they will go to the businesses that make it work in everyday jobs. SJT Digital helps you find where it genuinely earns its place, and gets the first thing working.

Where this is coming from

Almost nobody is as far along as they look.
That is the good news.

There is a great deal of confident talk about AI at the moment, and most of it is more confident than the work behind it. Looked at from the outside, every competitor seems further along. Your investor has probably asked the question. It is easy to feel late.

The view from inside is different. Spend time in enough of these businesses, of all sizes and in every sector, and the pattern is the same almost everywhere: a few people use it every day, most don't, someone has built something nobody else touches, and very few businesses can say what any of it has changed. What gets published is the tidy version. Very few businesses are as far along as their public story suggests, and once you know that, the gap feels a good deal smaller.

None of that makes AI any less real. It can now be applied well to almost any knowledge work in any business: finance, sales, admin, support, HR, the jobs that consist of moving information from one place to another. That is where the hours are. Where it has already landed, in the software teams of businesses that build software, a small group now gets through what used to need a department. The businesses that manage the same in their everyday work will be quicker and cheaper to run than the ones that don't, and the difference between them will not be the technology, which anyone can buy. It will be who made it work.

Making it work is a skill, and it is a different skill from being enthusiastic about it. It comes from having seen enough of these businesses from the inside to know what takes and what doesn't, and from getting the first real thing running rather than talking about the tenth. That is what SJT Digital brings.

What keeps turning out to be true

Four things that hold in most businesses.

It is no longer a software-company thing. The tools now sit inside the software everyone already uses, they read and write ordinary documents, and they do not need an engineer in the room to be useful. A finance team, a sales desk or an HR function can get real value from them this quarter. That was not true two years ago.

The best opportunities are hiding in ordinary processes. Ask a team where AI could help and the ideas come quickly. Look at the ones that would save the most hours and they are usually the same kind of thing: a job that was never quite written down, done slightly differently by everyone who does it. That used to be the reason nothing could be automated. Now writing the job down and having an assistant do it have become one piece of work, and it takes days. The opportunity is bigger than it looks, and it sits in the unglamorous places.

People who aren't engineers start building. Give a capable, curious person in an operations team the tools and they will build things that work, often faster than a formal project would. Then they reach the edge of what they can do safely without someone technical behind them. Both halves are predictable. The first is the opportunity; the second is what you plan for.

The ones that get somewhere pick one thing. One process, one person who owns it, a number written down before and after. Not a programme, not a platform, not a pilot in every department. The businesses that stall are the ones that tried to do it everywhere at once.

How an engagement takes shape

It starts with your question, not a package.

There is no standard programme here, because the businesses are not standard. What is right for a forty-person services firm with an overloaded finance team is not what is right for a two-hundred-person business with a product of its own and a customer support desk that never sleeps. So the shape of the work follows the business, and it is agreed in writing before anything starts.

It does always begin in the same place: with what your board or investor has actually asked for. "Do something about AI" is not a brief anyone can deliver against, and turning it into one is a real piece of work in itself.

Then time with the people doing the work. Not a survey, not a workshop: sitting with finance at month end, listening to how a quote gets put together, watching a support query get answered. That is where the genuine opportunities show themselves, and where the ones that only look like opportunities fall away.

What happens after that depends on what is found. Sometimes it is one thing built and put to work with the person who will own it, and a number to show for it. Sometimes it is a longer piece of work across several teams. Sometimes the most useful outcome is a short, plain view of where the business stands and what is worth doing next, which you act on yourselves. All three are legitimate results, and the right one is a conversation, not a menu.

One thing runs through all of it: the rules get written down. Customer contracts increasingly say what can and cannot go into an AI tool, and someone in your business is probably pasting client information into a personal account right now. Sorting that out is not the exciting part, but your FD will thank you for it, and it is the thing most businesses skip.
